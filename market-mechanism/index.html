Market equilibrium theory and price shocks

Understanding how markets establish equilibrium prices and allocate resources is fundamental to economic reasoning. Explore universal laws of supply and demand, root causes of market shocks, and market self-adjustment.

Market equilibrium

Market equilibrium state and self-stabilizing forces

Market equilibrium occurs when buyer intentions match seller intentions precisely. At the equilibrium price (PE), consumer quantity demanded equals producer quantity supplied (QD = QS = QE).

When P < PE: market shortage (excess demand)

Price is too low, so QD > QS. Buyers bid up prices until equilibrium is restored.

When P > PE: market surplus (excess supply)

Price is too high, so QS > QD. Unsold inventories force sellers to offer discounts until clearing.

Change in demand (non-price shifters)

D

A change in demand represents a parallel shift of the entire curve (D₀ → D₁) driven by non-price factors:

  • 1. Consumer incomes For normal goods, higher income increases demand. For inferior goods, higher income reduces demand.
  • 2. Prices of related goods A price hike for a substitute increases demand for the good. A price hike for a complement decreases demand.
  • 3. Tastes, fashions, and consumer information Advertising, health discoveries, or lifestyle shifts alter the willingness to buy.
  • 4. Market size and demographics Population expansion or demographic aging increases demand for specific product categories.
  • 5. Buyer expectations Anticipated future shortages or price surges induce preemptive current buying (current demand shifts right).
  • 6. Weather conditions, seasonality, and external shocks Summer heatwaves boost demand for cold refreshments; harsh winter freezes raise heating fuel demand.

Change in supply (non-price shifters)

S

A change in supply represents a shift of the entire curve (S₀ → S₁) determining producer quantities across all prices:

  • 1. Input costs (raw materials, wages, energy) Higher input costs inflate marginal costs, decreasing supply (shifts left).
  • 2. Technological progress and innovation More efficient machinery and automation cut unit costs, expanding supply outward.
  • 3. Producer taxes and government subsidies Higher excise duties shift supply left; grants and production subsidies shift supply right.
  • 4. Number of market sellers New competitors entering the sector expand total aggregate industry supply.
  • 5. Weather, nature, and external shocks Agricultural droughts or port blockages reduce supply; ideal weather expands harvests.

Four canonical market shifts (outcomes for PE and QE)

Equilibrium shifts summary under ceteris paribus

In summary
Increase in demand (D ↑) PE ↑   QE ↑

Demand shifts right. A temporary shortage emerges, bidding prices up. Outcome: both equilibrium price and quantity increase.

Decrease in demand (D ↓) PE ↓   QE ↓

Demand shifts left. A temporary surplus occurs, forcing price cuts. Outcome: both equilibrium price and quantity decrease.

Increase in supply (S ↑) PE ↓   QE ↑

Supply shifts right. Excess stock pushes prices down, enticing more consumption. Outcome: equilibrium price falls while quantity increases.

Decrease in supply (S ↓) PE ↑   QE ↓

Supply shifts left. Supply crunch creates a shortage, lifting prices. Outcome: equilibrium price increases while quantity falls.

Fundamental economic principle

Change in demand vs change in quantity demanded

One of the most frequent mistakes in microeconomics exams is confusing a movement along the curve with a shift of the entire curve.

1
1. Shift of the curve

“Change in demand” or “change in supply”

  • Cause: non-price determinants (consumer income, consumer tastes/fashions, prices of substitutes/complements, technology, production costs, taxes).
  • Graphical effect: the entire curve shifts outward to the right (increase) or inward to the left (decrease).
  • At every price level: consumers or producers wish to buy/sell a different quantity than before.
D0 → D1  |  S0 → S1  (slope remains constant!)
2
2. Movement along the curve

“Change in quantity demanded” or “quantity supplied”

  • Cause: exclusively a change in the good's own price, holding all else constant (ceteris paribus)!
  • Graphical effect: movement from one point to another along the exact same curve.
  • Mechanism: higher price = lower quantity demanded and higher quantity supplied.
Point A(P1, Q1) → Point B(P2, Q2) on D0

Market mechanism: what drives price changes? (root cause analysis)

In competitive markets, price never changes in a vacuum – it is determined by the interplay of demand and supply.

Shock transmission
A. Supply shift triggers movement along demand ΔS ⟹ movement along D

A bumper crop or lower costs causes supply to increase (S shifts right). A temporary surplus occurs, forcing sellers to lower prices. Buyers respond by purchasing more – resulting in a movement along the demand curve to the new equilibrium.

💡 Takeaway: shift of S changed the price, which forced demand to adjust via a movement along its curve.
B. Demand shift triggers movement along supply ΔD ⟹ movement along S

Rising incomes or consumer trends cause demand to surge (D shifts right). A shortage emerges, driving prices up. Seeing higher profits, producers ramp up production – resulting in a movement along the supply curve.

💡 Takeaway: shift of D changed the price, causing a movement along the supply curve.
When does this logic not apply? (exogenous price changes)
  • Government regulation (price controls): price floors or ceilings impose price shifts directly, driving movements along curves without any prior shift in market curves.
  • Monopoly power: a monopolist arbitrarily sets higher prices, forcing a movement along demand without competitive market equilibrating forces.
📌 Golden market rule: a shock on one side of the market (shift of curve A) alters the equilibrium price, which forces a movement along curve B.
Why must the shift be parallel (no 'anchoring')?
1. Parallel shift (fixed slope)

In linear model QD = b - a·P, a non-price factor alters only intercept b. Because slope parameter a is unchanged, the curve shifts strictly parallel without rotating or anchoring.

2. When does the slope change (rotation / pivot)?

The curve rotates around an intercept only when price elasticity/sensitivity changes (parameter a or c) – e.g. due to an ad-valorem tax or arrival of close substitutes.
